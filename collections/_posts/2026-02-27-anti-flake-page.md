I start things with ridiculous enthusiasm, & then just forget or abandon them.

At some point I realized I was carrying around this label in my head:

> *"I don’t do hard things."*  
> *"I have potential but I don’t do the hard work."*

If you've felt this, you know how much these labels hold you back.

So I added a page.

A page that functions like social pressure- but aimed at me.

## What it is

The Anti-Flake Page is a running log of things I start, and whether I finish them.

It has 3 states:

- **In Progress**- things I’m actively working on
- **On Hold**- things I’m not touching right now (but I’m not pretending they don’t exist)
- **Completed**- finished, done, shipped

And it has a few yearly counters (because my brain responds to numbers):

- **Shipped**
- **Learned**
- **Health**

Small wins count. A lot.

Because "I finished *something*" is the muscle I’m trying to build.

## How I’m trying to actually finish (not just “have goals”)

I’m borrowing three ideas from behavioral science and turning them into rules for this page:

### 1) If–then plans (so I don’t have to “feel like it”) [^1]
Big goals die because they require a daily decision. So I’m turning them into tiny defaults:

- **If** it’s **Tue/Thu/Sat at 8:30pm**, **then** I do **25 minutes of OSTEP**.
- **If** it’s **Sunday 11am**, **then** I do **30 minutes of life admin** (taxes, bills, forms).
- **If** I open my laptop after dinner, **then** I do **one concrete “next step”** on my current project.

The key is: the trigger decides for me.

### 2) Commitment devices (make quitting annoying) [^2]
This page is the commitment device. It works only if I keep it honest:

- **Max 5 items in progress.**[^3] If the list is full, I finish something before adding more.
- **On Hold is allowed**- but it stays visible. No pretending it didn’t happen.
- **Done needs a receipt.** A link, a date, a commit, a published post- *something that exists*.

Optional “hard mode” if I start slipping: put money on it (donate if I miss a deadline). I haven’t needed that yet, but it’s there.

### 3) Temptation bundling (pair “want” + “should”) [^4]
I’m making the “should” easier to start by attaching a small reward:

- My “OSTEP” sessions get a specific playlist / coffee / comfy spot.
- My Go learning gets a cafe session.
- Writing gets a “nice editor / no notifications” setup.
- The reward is **only for the work** (so it doesn’t turn into a free snack).

### The simple loop
- pick the **next finishable unit**
- tie it to a trigger (**if–then**)
- keep it visible (**anti-flake page**)
- require proof to count it (**receipt**)
- add a small treat to show up (**bundling**)

That’s the whole system: fewer decisions, fewer escape routes, more evidence.

[^1]: “Implementation intentions” (if–then plans) reliably improve follow-through because you pre-decide the when/where/how. See: Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-Analysis of Effects and Processes*.

[^2]: Commitment devices work by adding friction to quitting (hard commitments like money, and soft commitments like visibility/identity). See: Bryan, Karlan & Nelson (2010), *Commitment Devices*.

[^3]: Unfortunately capable of starting 10 things at once.

[^4]: “Temptation bundling” pairs an instantly fun “want” with a beneficial “should,” making it easier to start the should. See: Milkman et al. (2014), *Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling*.
